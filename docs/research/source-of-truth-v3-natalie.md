# **4\. Organizational origins and civil rights context**

## **4.1 1974 Confucius Plaza protests**

AAFE began as Asian Americans for Equal Employment during a campaign for construction jobs in Manhattan’s Chinatown. At the 764-apartment Confucius Plaza project, contractor DeMatteis Corp. refused to hire Asian workers. Young organizers drew on Black civil-rights activism and other local employment campaigns. \[S1, W1\]

On May 16, 1974, about 250 protesters entered the site and halted work. The demonstrations brought together older and younger residents, immigrants and U.S.-born participants, and people from different Asian backgrounds, with support from other communities. Founders recalled arrests and demonstrators physically blocking construction work. \[S1, W1\]

After eight weeks, the contractor signed a July 10 agreement to hire 27 minority workers, including Asian workers. The campaign established an organizing base that residents could approach about other problems. Its significance was both practical access to employment and a visible assertion of the community’s right to participate in decisions affecting its neighborhood. \[S1, W1\]

## **4.2 1977 Expansion beyond employment**

The organization changed its name to Asian Americans for Equality as its work expanded to tenant conditions and wider civil-rights advocacy. The later housing and community-development programs grew from this broader role. \[S1\]

## **4.3 1982 and 1983 Vincent Chin and the organized response**

Vincent Chin, a Chinese American draftsman in the Detroit area, was attacked by Ronald Ebens and Michael Nitz on June 19, 1982, during a period of anti-Asian scapegoating over auto-industry job losses. He died on June 23, shortly before his wedding. The attackers’ manslaughter sentences included probation and fines rather than imprisonment. \[S1, W3, W4\]

Lily Chin pressed for justice for her son. On March 31, 1983, more than 100 people and representatives of Asian organizations gathered in Detroit; the resulting American Citizens for Justice coalition crossed ethnic, occupational, generational, and political lines. It mobilized volunteers, donations, legal assistance, and civil-rights allies. On May 9 it organized a major demonstration in Detroit’s Kennedy Square. \[W3\]

The response expanded into national organizing and pressure for a federal civil-rights investigation. Ebens was initially convicted federally, but the conviction was overturned and he was acquitted at retrial. Neither attacker served prison time for the killing. The campaign strengthened pan-Asian advocacy and alliances with other communities while exposing barriers to equal protection. \[S1, W3, W4\]

Attribution limit: this is national civil-rights context. The reviewed materials do not establish a specific AAFE rally, legal campaign, or formal response to the Vincent Chin case. Do not attribute the Detroit coalition’s work to AAFE. \[S1\]

# **5\. Affordable housing and community development**

## **5.1 1983 Building organizational capacity**

AAFE hired Doris Koo as its first executive director in 1983\. Its programs subsequently combined neighborhood organizing with housing development, small-business assistance, and homeownership support. \[S1\]

## **5.2 1985 Fire and the Equality House response**

A 1985 Chinatown tenement fire killed two elderly residents and displaced 125 people. AAFE acquired city-owned buildings at 176 and 180 Eldridge Street to create 59 homes for low-income and formerly homeless residents. Equality House was a \$5.2 million rehabilitation involving Enterprise Community Partners and Fannie Mae. AAFE identifies it as New York City’s first affordable housing development using federal Low-Income Housing Tax Credits. \[S1, W2\]

Another fire damaged the project buildings during development. AAFE continued the rehabilitation, building experience in financing and preserving affordable housing that supported later projects. \[W2\]

## **5.3 1992 and 1997 Preservation at a larger scale**

Clinton/Peace Houses added 22 affordable units and Friendship Houses added 49 apartments; both were completed in 1992\. By 1997, AAFE reported rehabilitation of 11 buildings and 200 apartments. That year it also stepped in to finish renovations in nine Lower East Side buildings after another nonprofit developer closed. These projects helped establish capacity for later ground-up development. \[S1, W2\]

## **5.4 1996 to 1999 Broader community infrastructure**

AAFE opened a Flushing office in 1996\. Renaissance Economic Development Corporation became a community development financial institution in 1997, and AAFE Community Development Fund followed in 1999\. Housing work developed alongside lending and assistance for small businesses and prospective homeowners. \[S1\]

## **5.5 After 2001 Housing preservation and cooperative ownership**

Following September 11, AAFE used a Chinatown and Lower East Side acquisition program to preserve rent-stabilized housing. In 2013, it renovated 244 Elizabeth Street through the city’s Affordable Neighborhood Cooperative Program, enabling residents to buy their apartments. In 2018, AAFE opened One Flushing with HANAC and Monadnock Development, expanding its multigenerational affordable housing work in Queens. \[S1\]

The supplied history reports more than 1,400 apartments developed or preserved over three decades. It also identifies conflicting published totals for the acquisition program and One Flushing. Treat the aggregate as a figure reported in that research, and confirm precise project counts with AAFE before external publication. \[S1\]

# **7\. Daily operational source of truth**

The addendum records a user-stated direction: information arrives daily, and the hub should be the operational source of truth. Exact feed timing, integration methods, field authority, and failure ownership still require confirmation with AAFE. \[S4\]

## **7.1 Two meanings of source of truth**

## **7.2 Authority and record structure**

The proposed hub governs guest readiness, seat assignment, follow-up ownership, table and badge status, and check-in if adopted. Payment evidence remains authoritative in the approved payment or finance workflow; the hub displays payment information with its source and verification status. \[S4\]

Distinguish purchaser or sponsor contact, order or commitment, package entitlement, seat, and attendee. For example, a sponsor may have ten seats but only six named guests; the purchaser may not attend. Payment completion and guest-list completion are separate states. \[S3, S5, S6\]

## **7.3 Daily cycle in operating order**

1. Receive each agreed daily feed or staff-uploaded export; retain manual entry for checks, deposits, and comps.  
2. Validate records and match source identifiers to existing commitments and attendees. Flag uncertain matches, duplicates, and conflicting values.  
3. Safely update matched records, create genuinely new records, or place exceptions in a review queue. Preserve staff-approved corrections.  
4. Refresh counts for entitled, named, incomplete, and production-ready seats.  
5. Assign exceptions to an AAFE staff owner with a due date and next action.  
6. Show the last successful update for each feed and visibly flag late or failed processing.  
7. Produce timestamped exports for printing, backup, and check-in if included.

## **7.4 Acceptance criteria proposed by the addendum**

* Each incoming record records its source, source ID when available, import time, processing result, and any conflict.  
* Every import reports new, updated, rejected, and review-needed records.  
* A correction survives the next import unless an authorized resolution changes it; the original source value remains available.  
* The list and exports reflect the latest accepted changes at the displayed timestamp.  
* A late or failed feed is visible so stale data is not presented as current.

Daily does not automatically mean a live API connection. A controlled daily CSV upload can meet the initial requirement if timing, ownership, mapping, and failure handling are explicit. \[S4\]

## **13.6 What Give Lively cannot do today**

* **Comps for elected officials.** Give Lively’s own help center says its event ticketing does not support discount codes or complimentary tickets. Its recommended workaround is a hidden duplicate event page where staff enter each attendee by hand.  
* **Checks and direct deposits.** The same workaround applies to high volumes of offline sales. Offline donations can be recorded, but they are not tied to seats or guests.  
* **Guest names by package.** Per the intake, Give Lively cannot ask conditional questions based on how many seats a package includes, and sponsors cannot change guest names after adding them.  
* **What it does offer.** Attendee data can be downloaded as a CSV or shared as a live CSV link that refreshes every minute. That link is a realistic data feed for a companion tool, with no API required.

## **13.7 Why these gaps are not already filled**

The market has solved these problems, but only by replacing Give Lively. Paid gala platforms bundle ticketing, payments, seating, and check-in because they earn subscription and transaction fees on the whole event. None of them has a reason to build a free add-on for Give Lively, a free platform. Open-source platforms such as pretix and Hi.Events also expect to run the checkout themselves. The gap is an integration gap, not a missing feature: no product connects Give Lively sales, checks, direct deposits, and comps into one seat-by-seat guest list.

## **13.9 Weighing Aisling’s Rust-backed app**

Rust is a fast, memory-safe programming language with a strict compiler that catches many errors before the code runs. Those are real strengths, but a 500–600 guest list updated daily does not need Rust’s performance. The question is whether the app saves the team time.

* **In favor.** Working code already exists. If Aisling knows it well, she can move quickly in it. Rust’s type system makes certain bugs less likely.  
* **Against.** Rust has a steep learning curve. Team members who do not know it, including potentially the Backend Systems Lead, cannot contribute to the backend quickly. Iteration is usually slower than in TypeScript or Python for simple database and form work. After Demo Day, AAFE or volunteer maintainers are far less likely to find Rust skills than TypeScript or Python skills.  
* **Questions to answer in a one-hour walkthrough.** Does it run for another teammate from the README? Does its data model already separate purchaser, package, seat, and attendee? What share of the P0 requirements does it already cover? Does Cornell know Rust or want to learn it? Who owns the code, and does Aisling agree to reuse under Section 10?  
* **Recommended decision rule.** Adopt the app only if it already covers a large share of the P0 requirements and both backend owners are comfortable in Rust. Otherwise, reuse its interface ideas and any screens that fit, and build the backend in a language the whole team knows. Decide within the first week after the client meeting.

## **13.10 Cost and benefit: build, clone, or buy**

| Option | Year-one cash cost to AAFE | What AAFE gets | Trade-off |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Give Lively and spreadsheet (today) | No platform fee; processing fees only | Nothing new | Staff time and errors continue. |
| Give Lively and our companion hub (Path A or C) | No license; illustrative operating cost about \$1,500 per year (Section 9.3) | Keeps Give Lively; closes the comp, offline payment, and guest-name gaps | Needs a named owner, hosting, and support after Demo Day. |
| Forked open-source platform (Path B) | No license; hosting for a heavier application | A full ticketing system AAFE controls | Duplicates Give Lively; large codebase to maintain; AGPL obligations. |
| Hosted open-source (pretix, Hi.Events) | Per-ticket fees only, no subscription | Proven ticketing, check-in, and comps (pretix vouchers) | Replaces Give Lively checkout; per-ticket fees. |
| Mid-priced gala tool (ClickBid, RSVPify) | About \$900–\$1,500 per year, or per-ticket fees | Proven product with vendor support | Replaces or duplicates Give Lively; migration and training. |
| Premium gala platform (OneCause, GiveSmart) | About \$3,000 per year, or transaction fees | Full event-night service and support | Highest cost among gala tools. |
| Enterprise platform (Swoogo, Bizzabo, Cvent) | \$11,800 to \$25,000+ per year | Everything, and far more than needed | Built for many large events, not one annual gala. |

Team assessment: a custom hub is justified only if it keeps Give Lively, costs AAFE less per year than a mid-priced gala tool, and has a named owner after Demo Day. The strongest plan is Path A: copy the proven patterns from these tools in our own code, connect to Give Lively’s live CSV, and add what no tool offers for AAFE. If the conditions fail, recommending an existing product is the more honest outcome, and presenting this comparison openly will strengthen the team’s credibility either way.

## **13.11 What to copy and what to add**

* **Copy proven patterns.** Package-based seat allocation; purchaser separate from attendee; a private link for sponsors or table captains to add and edit guest names (GalaBid and RSVPify model); personal invitation links for comps (pretix model); table assignment; searchable check-in list by name, table, or QR code (ClickBid model); badge and CSV exports.  
* **Add what no tool offers for AAFE.** The Give Lively live CSV as the main feed; manual entry for checks and direct deposits with a verification status; a comp category that tracks RSVPs without payment; an AAFE relationship owner on every guest; a missing-information queue; duplicate and conflict flags across sources; staff-approved follow-up drafts.  
* **Do not build.** Payment processing, ticket checkout, auctions, or a donor CRM. Give Lively and Stripe keep doing those jobs.
